The Gospel Still Matters

Christian Universalism, Evangelism, and the New Birth

A biblical comparison with traditional Christian views

 

Introduction

Two questions arise naturally when Christian Universalism is presented: “If everyone is going to be saved, why preach the Gospel?” and “What about being born again?” These questions are important because they expose two common misunderstandings. First, Christian Universalism is not the claim that every person is already reconciled to God in experience, character, or fellowship. Second, the belief that God will ultimately reconcile all people does not make the Gospel unnecessary. Rather, it changes the way the purpose and scope of evangelism are understood.

Christian Universalism, as used in this study, means the conviction that the God revealed in Jesus Christ will finally accomplish His saving purpose for humanity: Christ will overcome sin and death, every knee will bow to Him, and God will become “all in all.” This conviction does not eliminate judgment, repentance, faith, conversion, discipleship, holiness, or the new birth. It places each of these realities within a larger confidence that God’s redemptive purpose will ultimately prevail.

Traditional Christian views are not completely uniform. Within historic Christianity there have been different understandings of election, free will, perseverance, the extent of Christ’s atonement, and the destiny of those who die without explicit faith in Christ. Nevertheless, the dominant evangelical framework ordinarily understands final salvation as limited to those who personally respond to the Gospel in faith, while Christian Universalism understands the final scope of Christ’s saving work as universal.

1. “If everyone is going to be saved, why preach the Gospel?”

The Christian Universalist answer

The short answer is that the Gospel is not merely a message about who gets into heaven. The Gospel is the proclamation of what God has done and is doing in Jesus Christ. It announces the reign of God, the victory of Christ, the forgiveness of sins, reconciliation, resurrection, and the renewal of creation. If God’s ultimate purpose is the reconciliation of all things to Himself through Christ, then proclaiming that good news is not pointless; it is participation in that very purpose.

Matthew 28:18–20 (WEB): “All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth. Go, and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you. Behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age.”[2]

The Great Commission therefore remains fully meaningful. Jesus does not command His followers merely to announce a future escape from punishment. He commands them to make disciples, baptize, teach, and form a people who live under His lordship. Universal reconciliation does not cancel discipleship; it gives discipleship a universal horizon.

1 Timothy 2:3–6 (WEB): “For this is good and acceptable in the sight of God our Savior, who desires all people to be saved and come to full knowledge of the truth. For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all; the testimony in its own times.”[3]

This passage places evangelism within God’s revealed desire for humanity. The Christian Universalist reads the universal language seriously: God desires all to be saved, Christ gave Himself for all, and the Church is therefore called to announce the good news to all. The certainty of God’s ultimate purpose does not make proclamation unnecessary any more than the certainty of Christ’s resurrection made the apostles’ preaching unnecessary.

The Gospel as proclamation, invitation, and participation

There are at least four reasons the Gospel remains necessary from a Christian Universalist perspective. First, people need to hear the truth about God. The Gospel reveals the character of God in Christ and confronts distorted pictures of God as arbitrary, distant, or hostile. Second, people are called to repentance and faith. Universal reconciliation does not mean that sin is harmless or that unbelief is spiritually inconsequential. Third, believers are called into present participation in the life of the coming kingdom. Salvation is not only a future destination; it is a present transformation. Fourth, the Church is sent to become an instrument of reconciliation, mercy, justice, healing, forgiveness, and witness.

2 Corinthians 5:18–20 (WEB): “But all things are of God, who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation; namely, that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not charging them with their trespasses, and having committed to us the word of reconciliation. We are therefore ambassadors on behalf of Christ, as though God were making his appeal through us. We beg you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.”[4]

The remarkable feature of this passage is the relationship between objective reconciliation and subjective response. Paul can proclaim that God was reconciling the world to Himself while simultaneously urging people to be reconciled to God. This provides an important theological pattern for Christian Universalism: God’s reconciling work does not make the human response meaningless. Rather, the Gospel summons people to enter consciously, joyfully, and obediently into the reconciliation God is accomplishing.

Why evangelism matters even if the final outcome is certain

A universalist theology can therefore distinguish between the certainty of God’s ultimate purpose and the urgency of the present human response. A parent who knows that a child will eventually understand a lesson does not stop teaching. A physician who expects a patient to recover does not stop administering treatment. Likewise, the Christian proclamation is one of the means through which God brings people from ignorance to knowledge, from alienation to fellowship, from bondage to freedom, and from death toward life.

Romans 10:13–15 (WEB): “For, ‘Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.’ How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? How will they believe in him whom they have not heard? How will they hear without a preacher? And how will they preach unless they are sent?”[5]

Paul’s reasoning assumes that proclamation matters because hearing, believing, calling upon the Lord, and experiencing salvation are genuinely related. Christian Universalism does not need to deny this relationship. It can instead deny the additional conclusion that the present opportunity to hear the Gospel is the only possible occasion on which God can bring a person to faith, or that failure to respond during this life necessarily establishes an endless state of separation.

Traditional Christian view

In the traditional evangelical framework, the question is often answered with an additional urgency: evangelism is necessary because people must hear and respond to the Gospel in order to be saved. On this view, the eternal destiny of the individual is ordinarily understood to depend upon whether the person dies reconciled to God through faith in Christ. The Great Commission is therefore both a summons to discipleship and a rescue mission.

John 3:16–18 (WEB): “For God so loved the world, that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life. For God didn’t send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him. He who believes in him is not judged. He who doesn’t believe has been judged already, because he hasn’t believed in the name of the one and only Son of God.”

Traditional readings emphasize the contrast between believing and not believing and the repeated biblical commands to evangelize. The strongest traditional form therefore reasons: if final salvation is limited to those who believe in Christ, and if the Gospel is the divinely appointed means of bringing people to faith, then preaching the Gospel is indispensable to the Church’s mission.

Christian Universalism agrees with the practical command to preach, but differs over the final theological conclusion. It interprets texts about judgment and unbelief as describing real judgment and real consequences without requiring the conclusion that God’s judgment will remain eternally successful in separating humanity from God.

2. “What about being ‘Born Again?’”

The Christian Universalist answer

Christian Universalism does not reject the new birth. On the contrary, Jesus’ teaching about being born again is central to Christian discipleship. The universalist question is not whether people need regeneration, but whether the necessity of regeneration requires the conclusion that God will permanently abandon everyone who has not experienced that transformation before physical death.

John 3:3–8 (WEB): “Jesus answered him, ‘Most certainly, I tell you, unless one is born anew, he can’t see God’s Kingdom.’ Nicodemus said to him, ‘How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother’s womb, and be born?’ Jesus answered, ‘Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born of water and spirit, he can’t enter into God’s Kingdom! That which is born of the flesh is flesh. That which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Don’t marvel that I said to you, “You must be born anew.” The wind blows where it wants to, and you hear its sound, but don’t know where it comes from and where it is going. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit.’[6]

The text plainly teaches the necessity of new birth for participation in the kingdom. A Christian Universalist should therefore avoid saying that everyone is automatically saved in the sense that everyone is already spiritually regenerated. Universalism concerns the final scope of God’s saving purpose; it does not erase the biblical distinction between flesh and Spirit, old creation and new creation, unbelief and faith, or alienation and communion.

Indeed, the new birth can be understood as one of the ways God’s universal saving purpose is realized. The promise is not merely that God will declare humanity acceptable without transformation. The promise is that God will actually bring creation into conformity with Christ.

2 Corinthians 5:17 (WEB): “Therefore if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new.”[8]

Titus 3:5 (WEB): “not by works of righteousness, which we did ourselves, but according to his mercy, he saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit,”

The new birth is therefore not a problem for Christian Universalism. It is part of the answer. The deeper question is when, how, and in what manner God brings every person into the life of the Spirit.

Born again as present participation in eternal life

The New Testament frequently speaks of eternal life not simply as endless duration but as a quality of life grounded in relationship with God. Jesus defines eternal life in relational terms: knowing the Father and the One whom He sent.

John 17:3 (WEB): “This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God, and him whom you sent, Jesus Christ.”[7]

From a Christian Universalist perspective, this means the new birth has enormous present significance. The believer does not merely wait for eventual universal reconciliation; the believer begins to experience reconciliation now. The Gospel calls people into communion with God in the present, into the life of the Spirit, into the body of Christ, and into the practical holiness of love.

Galatians 5:22–23 (WEB): “But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law.”

If all people will ultimately be reconciled to God, that does not mean that all people are presently living in the freedom of the Spirit. There remains an immense difference between being reconciled in God’s ultimate purpose and consciously participating in that reconciliation now.

Traditional Christian view

Traditional evangelical theology generally treats the new birth as the work of the Holy Spirit by which a sinner is spiritually regenerated and brought into saving faith in Christ. John 3 is central to this understanding. The new birth is not simply moral improvement or religious education; it is a work of God that changes the person’s spiritual condition.

In many traditional Protestant formulations, regeneration and conversion are closely connected to justification, faith, and incorporation into Christ. The precise order varies among theological traditions, but the common point is that human beings need divine grace and spiritual renewal rather than merely additional information.

Ephesians 2:8–10 (WEB): “for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared before that we would walk in them.”

Traditional Christianity therefore has strong biblical grounds for insisting that universal salvation cannot be reduced to the idea that everyone is saved regardless of faith, repentance, or transformation. The disagreement with Christian Universalism is principally about the final extent of God’s saving work and the duration and purpose of judgment, not about whether regeneration is taught.

Does being born again happen only in this life?

This is where the two frameworks diverge more sharply. Traditional evangelical theology normally regards the present life as the decisive period for conversion. Christian Universalists who affirm postmortem reconciliation argue that Scripture’s universal promises should not be restricted by an assumption that death ends every possibility of repentance, faith, or divine restoration.

Philippians 2:9–11 (WEB): “Therefore God also highly exalted him, and gave to him the name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, those on earth, and those under the earth, and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.”[9]

The Christian Universalist reads this universal confession together with texts that describe God’s purpose as reconciliation and the eventual subjection of all things to Christ. The question is whether every knee bows freely and savingly or merely under compelled acknowledgment. Christian Universalists argue that the biblical picture of God’s victory is better understood as ultimately restorative rather than as an eternal victory in which countless creatures remain forever alienated.

1 Corinthians 15:22–28 (WEB): “For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. But each in his own order: Christ the first fruits, then those who are Christ’s, at his coming. Then the end, when he will deliver up the Kingdom to God, even the Father; when he will have abolished all rule and all authority and power. For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy that will be abolished is death. For, ‘He put all things in subjection under his feet.’ But when he says, ‘All things are put in subjection,’ it is evident that he is excepted who subjected all things to him. When all things have been subjected to him, then the Son will also himself be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all.”[10]

This passage is particularly important to Christian Universalism because Paul does not merely describe the survival of believers. He describes the progressive conquest of Christ’s enemies, the destruction of death, the subjection of all things, and the final state in which God is “all in all.” Traditional interpreters may understand “all” in more restricted contexts or distinguish the defeat of enemies from their salvation. Christian Universalists argue that the climactic movement of the passage points toward comprehensive restoration.

What Christian Universalism does—and does not—mean

Christian Universalism should not be confused with the proposition that every religion is equally true, that sin does not matter, that repentance is unnecessary, or that everyone is presently in fellowship with God. A biblical universalism can affirm that Jesus Christ is the unique mediator of salvation, that the cross and resurrection are essential, that the Holy Spirit regenerates, that repentance is necessary, and that judgment is real.

Its distinctive claim is about the final reach of Christ’s victory. The universalist expects God’s grace ultimately to accomplish what God intends: the reconciliation of creation, the defeat of death, and the restoration of humanity in Christ.

Colossians 1:19–20 (WEB): “For all the fullness was pleased to dwell in him; and through him to reconcile all things to himself, by him, whether things on the earth or things in the heavens, having made peace through the blood of his cross.”[11]

This is why evangelism remains essential. The Gospel is the announcement of the reconciliation God is accomplishing through Christ. The Church does not preach because God might otherwise lose His battle. The Church preaches because God has entrusted believers with the ministry of reconciliation and calls them to participate in the life and mission of His Son.

A practical theological synthesis

The two questions can therefore be answered together. If everyone will ultimately be saved, why preach the Gospel? Because the Gospel is the means by which people hear about Christ, encounter the truth, repent, believe, enter discipleship, receive the Spirit, and participate in God’s reconciling work now. And what about being born again? It remains essential because final reconciliation is not merely a legal change in status; God’s saving purpose includes genuine renewal and communion with Him.

Traditional Christianity and Christian Universalism therefore share much more ground than the first question may suggest. Both affirm the authority of Scripture, the uniqueness of Christ, the necessity of grace, the call to repentance and faith, the reality of the new birth, the mission of the Church, and the importance of holy living. The decisive difference concerns the final scope of Christ’s saving victory and the meaning and duration of judgment.

For Christian Universalism, this difference should not weaken evangelistic urgency. It should purify it. The Church can proclaim Christ not as a means of manipulating people through terror, but as good news: God has acted in Christ to reconcile, heal, liberate, and restore. The invitation to faith is therefore an invitation into reality rather than merely an escape from punishment. The believer is called to experience now what God will ultimately accomplish in the whole creation—the triumph of divine love, holiness, reconciliation, and life.

Conclusion

The Gospel remains worth preaching precisely because it is good news. Christian Universalism does not make evangelism obsolete; it places evangelism inside the larger biblical story of God’s victorious purpose in Christ. The new birth likewise remains indispensable. Jesus did not teach that human beings simply need a change of destination. He taught that they need new life from above.

The Christian Universalist hope is therefore not that God will save humanity without transformation, but that God will ultimately accomplish the transformation toward which the Gospel points. The Church announces that hope, invites people to enter it now, and lives as a community in which the reconciliation of all things is already being anticipated.
