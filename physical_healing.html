Introduction: Healing within the Gospel of Restoration

A Christian Universalist theology of physical healing begins with a larger question: What is God ultimately doing in Christ? If the gospel announces merely the rescue of selected souls from an otherwise permanent condition of death and separation, healing may appear as an occasional interruption of the present order. But if the gospel announces the restoration of creation through Christ, then physical healing belongs naturally within the larger biblical movement from creation, through redemption, to resurrection and renewal.[1]

This perspective does not mean that every Christian will be physically healed during the present age. Nor does it mean that sickness can be explained by inadequate faith, hidden sin, or divine rejection. Rather, Christian Universalism can affirm two truths simultaneously: God genuinely heals in the present, and God's ultimate victory over death is more comprehensive than any healing experienced before death.

1. The Father: The Source and Purpose of Restoration

The Father stands at the beginning and end of the biblical story. Creation originates in God, reconciliation proceeds from God's purpose, and the final goal is God's presence filling creation. From this standpoint, physical healing is not foreign to the character of God. It reflects, however imperfectly and temporarily, the Creator's intention to bring life out of death and order out of corruption.

“For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life.” — John 3:16, (WEB)

The Father's love is directed toward the world. A Universalist reading emphasizes that salvation must finally be understood in harmony with the breadth of that divine purpose. The Father does not merely preserve disembodied existence; Scripture's hope culminates in the renewal of creation and the defeat of death.

“For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy that will be destroyed is death.” — 1 Corinthians 15:25–26, (WEB)

2. The Son: Jesus as the Revelation of God's Healing Kingdom

Jesus makes the Father's restorative character visible. His ministry includes proclamation of the kingdom, forgiveness of sins, deliverance from oppression, and physical healing. These works should not be reduced to isolated demonstrations of supernatural power. They are signs of the kingdom breaking into the present age.[3]

“Jesus went about all the cities and the villages, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness among the people.” — Matthew 9:35, (WEB)

Jesus's healings also point beyond themselves. Lazarus was raised and later died again; those healed in Galilee and Judea eventually faced ordinary mortality. The miracles were signs of something greater—the final defeat of death.

“I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies. Whoever lives and believes in me will never die.” — John 11:25–26, (WEB)

3. The Holy Spirit: Present Participation in Resurrection Life

The Holy Spirit connects present Christian experience with the future restoration. The Spirit is God's present life-giving presence, and Paul explicitly connects that presence with the future life of the mortal body.

“But if the Spirit of him who raised up Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised up Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you.” — Romans 8:11, (WEB)

This is a remarkable statement for a theology of healing. Paul does not separate the Spirit's work from embodiment. The Spirit who raised Jesus is the Spirit who dwells in believers and who will give life to their mortal bodies. Physical healing in the present can therefore be understood as a foretaste of the Spirit's larger resurrection work.[4]

4. Creation Groans: Why Healing Is Not Yet Universal in Experience

Romans 8 prevents an overly simplistic theology of healing. The same believers who possess the Spirit still suffer, groan, and await the redemption of their bodies. The Spirit's presence is therefore compatible with an unfinished creation.[5]

“For we know that the whole creation groans and travails in pain together until now. Not only so, but ourselves also, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for adoption, the redemption of our body.” — Romans 8:22–23, (WEB)

The phrase “first fruits” is crucial. The Spirit represents the beginning of the harvest, not the harvest's completion. Christians can experience healing, strength, peace, and renewal now while still inhabiting mortal bodies subject to decay.[2]

5. Healing, Faith, and the Problem of Blame

A Christian Universalist perspective can reject a simplistic equation between faith and guaranteed immediate physical recovery. The New Testament contains extraordinary healings and faithful believers who continued to experience bodily weakness or illness.

“Stop drinking only water, but use a little wine for your stomach’s sake and your frequent illnesses.” — 1 Timothy 5:23, (WEB)
“I left Trophimus at Miletus sick.” — 2 Timothy 4:20, (WEB)

This matters pastorally. When healing does not occur, the sufferer should not be burdened with the accusation that insufficient faith caused the illness to remain. A theology of universal restoration can preserve confident prayer while protecting the dignity of those who continue to suffer.[6]

6. The Church's Prayer for the Sick

The New Testament gives the church explicit permission—even encouragement—to pray for physical healing. James instructs the elders to pray for the sick, and the practice assumes that God may respond through healing.[7]

“Is any among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer of faith will heal him who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up.” — James 5:14–15, (WEB)

The Christian Universalist need not weaken this promise. Instead, it can be placed within the broader theology of the kingdom. We pray because God is good, because Christ has demonstrated compassion toward the sick, and because the Spirit is the giver of life. We also pray without pretending to know exactly how or when God will answer a particular petition.

7. Present Healing as a Foretaste of Universal Restoration

The distinctive contribution of Christian Universalism is its insistence that the final horizon of salvation is larger than the healing of individuals during earthly life. The same Christ who heals individuals is presented as the one through whom God reconciles all things.[8]

“For all the fullness was pleased to dwell in him, and through him to reconcile all things to himself by him, whether things on the earth or things in the heavens, having made peace through the blood of his cross.” — Colossians 1:19–20, (WEB)

The phrase “all things” provides the cosmic setting for Christian hope. A present healing is therefore not the whole promise. It is a sign of the greater reconciliation toward which the gospel moves.

8. Resurrection: The Ultimate Healing of Mortality

First Corinthians 15 provides the decisive eschatological framework. Paul's argument is not that Christians merely possess immortal souls. He speaks of resurrection and transformation. The enemy finally defeated is death itself.[9]

“For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive. But each in his own order: Christ the first fruits, then those who are Christ’s, at his coming. Then the end comes, when he will deliver up the Kingdom to God, even the Father; when he will have abolished all rule and all authority and power. For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet. The last enemy that will be destroyed is death.” — 1 Corinthians 15:22–26, (WEB)

For Christian Universalism, “in Christ all will be made alive” belongs beside the wider Pauline vision of God's final triumph. Universal reconciliation does not mean that every person necessarily experiences every blessing at the same chronological moment. It means that God's restorative purpose is ultimately victorious over sin, corruption, and death.

“But when all things have been subjected to him, then the Son will also himself be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all.” — 1 Corinthians 15:28, (WEB)

9. The Father, Son, and Spirit in a Unified Theology of Healing

The doctrine of the Trinity provides a coherent way to hold these themes together. The Father is the source and goal of restoration; the Son is the incarnate revelation and agent of restoration; and the Spirit is the present giver and mediator of resurrection life. Physical healing therefore belongs within the unified work of the triune God.[10]

The Father's love provides the purpose. The Son's ministry reveals the character of that purpose. The Spirit makes the life of that purpose present within believers and anticipates its consummation. What is seen in Jesus's healing ministry is therefore not an isolated divine activity but an earthly manifestation of the same restorative movement that culminates in resurrection and reconciliation.

10. A Balanced Christian Universalist Theology of Present Healing

A mature Christian Universalist theology can affirm several propositions simultaneously. God is genuinely compassionate toward bodily suffering. Jesus's healing ministry provides biblical warrant for expecting God to act in the present. The Holy Spirit is already the life-giving presence of the age to come. Christians remain within a creation that groans, and therefore not every sickness is immediately removed. The failure to receive immediate healing should never become a basis for condemning the sufferer. Finally, the ultimate promise of God is greater than present healing: it is resurrection and the final defeat of death.[11]

This approach permits bold prayer without presumption. A Christian may pray, “Father, heal this body,” with genuine expectation and trust. At the same time, the Christian can say, “Your ultimate purpose is life, and death will not have the final word.” Such hope does not diminish present suffering; it places present suffering inside the larger story of redemption.

11. Unanswered Prayer, Ongoing Suffering, and Physical Death

A Christian Universalist theology of healing must take seriously the biblical reality that faithful prayer is sometimes followed by continued suffering rather than immediate recovery. The New Testament does not present unanswered prayer as proof that God is absent, that the sufferer has failed, or that healing is impossible. Instead, Scripture places unanswered or apparently unanswered prayer within the larger tension between the kingdom already present and the redemption that has not yet been fully manifested.

“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” — Matthew 27:46; Psalm 22:1, (WEB)

Jesus himself gives the church language for lament. The cry of abandonment is not unbelief; it is prayer spoken from within profound suffering. The biblical witness therefore permits Christians to bring confusion, grief, disappointment, and unanswered petitions honestly before God. Prayer does not require the sufferer to pretend that everything is already healed.[13]

Unanswered Prayer Does Not Mean Divine Absence

Jesus teaches his disciples to pray persistently, which implies that prayer may involve waiting. The parable of the persistent widow is not a promise that every petition will be answered according to the petitioner's timetable. It is an invitation to continue trusting God's justice and goodness.[14]

“He also spoke a parable to them that they must always pray, and not give up.” — Luke 18:1, (WEB)

Paul provides an even more personal example. He repeatedly prayed concerning his 'thorn in the flesh,' asking that it be removed, yet the answer he received was not immediate physical relief. Instead, God spoke of sufficient grace and power manifested in weakness. Whatever the precise nature of Paul's affliction, the passage establishes an important principle: faithful prayer and continued suffering can coexist.[15]

“My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.” — 2 Corinthians 12:9, (WEB)

This does not mean that suffering is itself God's preferred end for the believer. Rather, it means that God's grace can remain active when the requested healing does not occur. From a Universalist perspective, this is particularly important because God's ultimate restorative purpose is not negated by the incompleteness of present experience.

Ongoing Suffering and the Groaning of Creation

Romans 8 provides perhaps the clearest theological framework for ongoing suffering. Creation groans, believers groan, and the Spirit himself intercedes within that groaning. The presence of the Spirit therefore does not mean that Christians have already entered the final condition of creation.[16]

“For we know that the whole creation groans and travails in pain together until now. Not only so, but ourselves also, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for adoption, the redemption of our body.” — Romans 8:22–23, (WEB)

The expression 'first fruits of the Spirit' is central. Christians possess something genuinely eschatological now, but they possess it as the beginning of the harvest. Healing, therefore, may occur as a genuine manifestation of God's kingdom without implying that all suffering must disappear before the resurrection. The Christian lives between promise and fulfillment.

Physical Death and the Defeat of Death

Physical death presents the deepest challenge to a theology of present healing. Even when a person is healed of a particular disease, mortality remains. The New Testament therefore directs Christian hope beyond the indefinite extension of earthly life toward resurrection. Jesus does not merely promise that his followers will avoid dying; he identifies himself as the resurrection and the life.

“I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies.” — John 11:25, (WEB)

For Christian Universalism, this distinction becomes decisive. The ultimate Christian answer to death is not simply that some individuals will be granted longer biological life. It is that death itself will finally be defeated. Paul's resurrection argument culminates in the declaration that death is the last enemy to be destroyed.[17]

“The last enemy that will be destroyed is death.” — 1 Corinthians 15:26, (WEB)

This places physical death within the larger victory of Christ. Death is real, painful, and presently powerful, but it is not ultimate. Because Christ's resurrection is the first fruits, the resurrection of humanity belongs to the same divine purpose. A Christian Universalist reading extends this hope toward the comprehensive reconciliation described elsewhere in Paul: God's restorative purpose finally reaches 'all things' and culminates in God being 'all in all.'

“For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive.” — 1 Corinthians 15:22, (WEB)
“But when all things have been subjected to him, then the Son will also himself be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all.” — 1 Corinthians 15:28, (WEB)

The Universalist Hope in the Face of Non-Healing

This framework changes the pastoral meaning of unanswered prayer. If someone prays for healing and remains sick, the Christian response need not be to assign blame. The sufferer may continue to pray, seek appropriate medical care, receive the ministry of the church, lament honestly, and hope in God. If death eventually comes, the Christian hope does not terminate at the grave.

Christian Universalism therefore does not require a choice between believing that God heals today and believing that God will ultimately heal creation. Both can be affirmed, provided the two are distinguished. Present healing is provisional and anticipatory; resurrection is final and consummating. Present suffering is real; it is not God's final word. Physical death is unavoidable within the present age; it is not the final destiny of creation.

“Behold, God’s dwelling is with people, and he will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes. Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore. The first things have passed away.” — Revelation 21:3–4, (WEB)

The Christian Universalist theology of healing can therefore speak with both realism and hope. It does not deny the pain of the present age, and it does not turn every unanswered prayer into a theological puzzle that must be solved by blaming the sufferer. Instead, it looks to the character of God revealed in Christ, the present presence of the Spirit, and the final promise of resurrection and restoration. The prayer 'Lord, heal me' remains meaningful even when the answer is delayed or seems absent, because the deepest Christian confidence is that God's life-giving purpose will ultimately overcome sickness, corruption, and death.[18]

Conclusion: Healing as a Sign of the Victorious Gospel

From a Christian Universalist perspective, physical healing belongs within the victorious movement of the gospel from creation to new creation. The Father is not indifferent to the suffering of creation. The Son has entered bodily existence, healed the sick, died, and risen. The Holy Spirit dwells in believers as the first fruits of resurrection life. Consequently, when healing occurs in the present, it can be received with gratitude as a sign of the kingdom already breaking into the world.

But the Christian hope does not end with temporary recovery. The deepest promise is the redemption of the body and the destruction of death. Present healing is therefore a sign that points beyond itself. It is a foretaste of what God intends to accomplish completely.

The Universalist confession is ultimately not that every disease will disappear whenever we ask, but that disease, corruption, and death will not have the final word. God's restorative purpose in Christ is larger than the limits of the present age. The Spirit who gives life now is the Spirit who will finally bring creation into the fullness of God's life.[12]
