The Victorious Gospel

An Exegetical Theology of Christian Universalism

Preface

This book is an attempt to read the Christian Scriptures as Christian Scripture: canonically, historically, literarily, and theologically. It begins with the conviction that the doctrine of salvation cannot be isolated from the doctrine of God, the identity of Jesus Christ, the vocation of the church, or the biblical account of the goal of history. The question of universal salvation therefore appears here not as an appendix to the doctrine of hell but as a question about the scope and purpose of God’s work in Christ.

The manuscript follows the supplied twelve-part outline, retaining its movement from Scripture, through sin and Christology, salvation, death and resurrection, kingdom, church, sacraments, ministry and Christian life, to eschatology. The governing thesis is stated cautiously: the biblical canon supplies substantial positive grounds for believing that God’s saving purpose in Christ finally encompasses every rational creature, while the traditional texts concerning judgment must be interpreted without being allowed to contradict that larger canonical witness. This is not an argument that every difficult text is easy. It is an argument that difficult texts should be read within the whole grammar of the gospel.

The World English Bible (WEB) is used for Scripture quotations. Scripture references without quotation marks are paraphrases or allusions. The aim is neither to conceal contrary readings nor to make the conclusion depend upon one translation. Greek and Hebrew observations are therefore used where they illuminate the argument, but theological claims are repeatedly tested against literary context, canonical intertextuality, and the actual shape of the New Testament proclamation.

Introduction: What Exegetical Theology Is Doing Here

Exegetical theology stands between close reading and doctrinal synthesis. It is not merely a verse-by-verse commentary, because the interpreter asks what a text means within the argument of a book and within the canon. Nor is it systematic theology detached from the particulars of Scripture. The controlling discipline is the text itself: its words, syntax, rhetoric, historical setting, literary form, intertextual echoes, and place in the canonical witness.

A Christian doctrine of universal reconciliation must therefore survive several tests. It must account for the universality of creation, the universality of divine love, the universality of Christ’s lordship, the universality of resurrection, and the universal scope of the kingdom’s consummation. At the same time it must account honestly for texts concerning wrath, exclusion, destruction, punishment, Gehenna, the “eternal” or “everlasting” fire, and the final judgment. It is inadequate simply to collect universalist proof texts while ignoring these passages; it is equally inadequate to collect judgment texts while treating the universal language of salvation as rhetorical decoration.

The biblical question is especially sharp because Scripture speaks of God’s purposes in absolute language. God “desires all people to be saved and come to full knowledge of the truth” (1 Timothy 2:4). Christ is “the Savior of all men, especially of those who believe” (1 Timothy 4:10). Through Christ God purposes “to reconcile all things to himself” (Colossians 1:20). Paul announces that “as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive” (1 Corinthians 15:22). The risen Christ declares, “I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself” (John 12:32). The question is whether such statements describe God’s actual final accomplishment or only an intention perpetually frustrated by creatures.

That question cannot be answered by one word. It requires a theology of God and creation. If God is the Creator of all, then creation is not an accidental population of independent beings. If the Son became human for the salvation of humanity, his assumption of human nature cannot be treated as a merely private transaction for a minority. If the Spirit is the giver of life, the eschatological work of the Spirit cannot terminate in the permanent triumph of death over most of those whom God made. The doctrine of universal reconciliation, if true, is therefore a consequence of a network of doctrines rather than a single isolated proposition.

At the same time, Christian universalism must not become sentimental universalism. Scripture does not minimize sin. It calls sin slavery, blindness, death, hostility to God, and guilt. Judgment is real. Divine wrath is real. Repentance is necessary. The cross is not a decorative symbol of divine niceness but the place where the gravity of evil and the depth of divine love meet. Universalism, in its classical Christian form, does not say that salvation is automatic because sin does not matter. It says that grace is finally stronger than sin because Christ’s saving work is finally effective.

The central methodological question is consequently this: when Scripture gives us both universal and particular statements about salvation and judgment, what canonical synthesis best accounts for both? The chapters that follow attempt to answer that question without pretending that the evidence is one-dimensional.

Chapter 1. The Bible: Its Nature and Purpose

The word “Bible” names a collection of writings, but Christian faith treats those writings as a canon: a received rule or standard through which the church hears the apostolic and prophetic witness. The Old Testament is not a preliminary religious book superseded by the New Testament. It is the Scripture of Jesus and the apostles, the matrix within which the New Testament’s claims about Messiah, kingdom, covenant, resurrection, judgment, and restoration become intelligible.

Jesus repeatedly treats Israel’s Scriptures as authoritative. In the wilderness he answers temptation with “It is written.” After the resurrection he interprets “all the Scriptures” as bearing witness to the Messiah (Luke 24:27). Paul can call the sacred writings “inspired by God” and useful for teaching, reproof, correction, and instruction in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16). The authority of Scripture is therefore not merely an abstract property of a book. Scripture exercises authority by making God’s purposes known and by forming a people capable of faithful obedience.1

The goal of Scripture is likewise larger than information. Psalm 19 moves from God’s self-disclosure in creation to the perfection of Torah. The prophetic writings summon Israel toward covenant fidelity and anticipate a renewed creation. The Gospels proclaim the kingdom of God in the person and work of Jesus. The epistles instruct communities whose existence is evidence that the Messiah has begun the work of new creation. Revelation ends not with souls escaping the world but with the holy city, the nations, the river of life, the tree of life, and the healing of the nations.

This canonical trajectory matters for universalism because Scripture’s final vision is not the abandonment of creation. The goal is restoration. Acts 3:21 speaks of “the the victorious gospel” spoken through the holy prophets.2 The Greek phrase apokatastasis pantōn is unusually broad. It does not by itself settle every question about who is saved, but it establishes restoration as an explicitly biblical category for describing the eschatological work of God.

The Old Testament provides the conceptual grammar. Abraham is promised that “all of the families of the earth will be blessed” through him (Genesis 12:3). Psalm 22 ends with all the ends of the earth remembering and turning to Yahweh. Isaiah repeatedly moves from Israel’s restoration toward the inclusion of the nations: “All flesh will come to worship before me” (Isaiah 66:23). The servant is appointed as a light to the nations “that you may be my salvation to the end of the earth” (Isaiah 49:6). These texts do not erase Israel’s election; they explain its missionary and eschatological purpose.

A responsible reading must also recognize genre. Poetry uses parallelism and imagery; prophecy may compress temporal horizons; apocalyptic literature communicates through symbols; wisdom literature generalizes from ordinary experience. A theological conclusion should therefore arise from patterns across genres rather than from a mechanical tally of isolated sentences.

The authority of Scripture does not remove the need for interpretation. It creates the need. The interpreter must ask what a word meant, what a writer intended, how a passage functions rhetorically, and how later Scripture reuses earlier Scripture. “Eternal” cannot be interpreted responsibly by a dictionary alone; neither can “all.” The semantic range of a word is constrained by context. The canon then constrains the synthesis.

The universalist thesis is strongest when it is not imposed upon Scripture from outside but discovered through Scripture’s own movement. Creation is universal. The promise to Abraham is universal in horizon. The Messiah’s reign is universal in scope. The Spirit is poured out upon all flesh. The resurrection is universal. Judgment is universal. And the final biblical image is the healing of the nations. Universal reconciliation therefore asks whether the final stage of this narrative remains as broad as its beginning.

“For the word of God is living and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the dividing of soul and spirit, of both joints and marrow, and is able to discern the thoughts and intentions of the heart.” (Hebrews 4:12, WEB)

The interpreter must therefore submit not merely to texts that seem congenial but to the whole canon. Christian universalism is defensible only if it can remain under Scripture’s judgment while reading Scripture as a unified witness to God’s saving purpose.

Chapter 2. The Plight of Man as Sinner

The biblical diagnosis of humanity is darker than modern moral psychology often permits. Sin is not simply the presence of bad acts among otherwise autonomous and morally neutral agents. It is a condition of bondage. Humanity is described as enslaved to sin (John 8:34), captive to the “god of this age” (2 Corinthians 4:4), dead in trespasses and sins (Ephesians 2:1), hostile to God (Romans 8:7), and unable to boast before God (Romans 3:27).

The universality of sin is explicit. “For all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God” (Romans 3:23). Paul does not use this universality merely to produce guilt. He uses it to establish the universality of grace. The same chapter moves from the universal plight to the universal availability of justification by God’s grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.

The language of Adam is particularly important.3 Romans 5 does not present Adam merely as the first sinner in a chronological series. Adam is a representative head whose disobedience has consequences for the many. Paul then constructs a deliberate contrast: “For as through one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, even so through one Man’s obedience many will be made righteous” (Romans 5:19). The parallelism is not mathematically simplistic, but neither is the second “many” casually weaker than the first. Paul’s concern is to show that the reign of death introduced through Adam is decisively answered by the reign of grace through Christ.

First Corinthians 15 develops the same pattern in resurrection form: “For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive” (15:22). The context is not abstract moralism but bodily resurrection. Paul’s “all” is eschatological and corporate. Yet it is also strikingly symmetrical. Death’s universality in Adam is answered by life “in Christ.”

This raises the problem of freedom. If humans are truly free, how can salvation be universal? The question depends upon what freedom means. Scripture does not define freedom as the metaphysical power to remain eternally opposed to God. Jesus says, “If therefore the Son makes you free, you will be free indeed” (John 8:36). Freedom is liberation into truth and goodness. A person enslaved to sin is not more free because the chains are called choices.

The biblical account of Satan and demons intensifies the diagnosis. The devil is portrayed as deceiver, accuser, tempter, and enemy. Yet even here the New Testament refuses dualism. Satan is not God’s equal. The cross is the decisive judgment upon the powers: Christ “disarmed the principalities and the powers, and made a public spectacle of them” (Colossians 2:15). Hebrews says that through death Christ destroyed “him who had the power of death, that is, the devil” (Hebrews 2:14).

If death itself is an enemy to be destroyed, then eternal death as an everlasting ontological rival to God’s life would sit uneasily with Paul’s climactic declaration, “The last enemy that will be destroyed is death” (1 Corinthians 15:26). Christian universalism does not require denying that Scripture teaches final judgment; it requires asking whether judgment has the final word or whether judgment is itself subordinated to God’s victory over death and sin.

The biblical picture of guilt must be preserved. Sin is not merely illness, though it is a kind of sickness; it is not merely ignorance, though ignorance contributes to it; it is not merely social conditioning, though social structures participate in it. Sin is rebellion. Yet guilt and bondage coexist. Paul can say both that humans are responsible and that they are enslaved. This combination prevents two opposite errors: moralism, which imagines that sinners simply need better information, and fatalism, which imagines that sinners are therefore beyond responsibility or redemption.

Universal reconciliation, if true, must therefore be a doctrine of liberation.4 The Savior does not simply announce that God has decided not to count sins. He breaks bondage, forgives guilt, heals the will, and raises the dead. Salvation is not God changing the legal status of creatures while leaving their hearts untouched. It is God’s work of making enemies into children.

The severity of the plight makes the universal scope of salvation more, not less, significant. If sin is genuinely slavery and death, then the gospel must eventually be able to say that slavery has ended and death has been overcome. Otherwise the final victory remains partial.

“But God, being rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ.” (Ephesians 2:4–5, WEB)

The doctrine of sin therefore provides the problem to which the doctrine of universal reconciliation must be the answer. It is not enough to say that God wants all to be saved. The gospel must finally explain how the power that holds humanity can actually be broken.

Chapter 3. The Christology of the New Testament

Christian universalism stands or falls with Christology. The question is not simply whether enough people will be saved. The prior question is who Jesus Christ is and what his person means for the creation.

The New Testament presents Jesus with titles that converge upon divine authority and saving mission. He is Messiah, Son of God, Son of Man, Lord, Savior, High Priest, mediator, Lamb of God, and Logos. These titles should not be flattened into synonyms. Each carries a scriptural and theological history.

“Messiah” identifies Jesus as Israel’s anointed king. His kingship is not tribal in its final horizon. Psalm 2 anticipates the nations as his inheritance. Daniel 7 portrays the Son of Man receiving dominion over peoples, nations, and languages. The resurrection therefore cannot be reduced to private vindication; it is God’s enthronement of the crucified Messiah.

The title “Lord” is especially significant because the New Testament can apply to Jesus language drawn from Israel’s confession of the one God.5 Philippians 2:9–11 culminates in every knee bowing and every tongue confessing that Jesus Christ is Lord, “to the glory of God the Father.” The confession is universal, but the text does not portray it as merely forced speech. It is the fulfillment of Isaiah 45:23, where every knee bows to Yahweh. The Christological implication is enormous: the universal acknowledgment of Jesus belongs within the universal acknowledgment of Israel’s God.

The title “Savior” has a similarly expansive force. Luke’s infancy narrative announces “good news of great joy which will be to all the people” (Luke 2:10). Simeon sees Jesus as “a light for revelation to the nations” (Luke 2:32). The Johannine Jesus is “the Savior of the world” (John 4:42). First John says that Christ is “the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not for ours only, but also for the whole world” (1 John 2:2). The cumulative effect is difficult to confine to an arbitrarily small group.

Christ as mediator intensifies the point. First Timothy 2:5–6 says there is “one God, and one mediator between God and men, Christ Jesus, himself man, who gave himself as a ransom for all.” The universal “all” is structurally related to the statement in verse 4 that God desires all to be saved. If the ransom is given for all, the exegete must ask whether the ransom’s purpose is finally effective.

The Logos theology of John 1 places salvation within creation itself.6 “All things were made through him” (John 1:3). The one through whom all things came to be enters the world as flesh. The Creator does not save by abandoning his creation but by entering it. John 1:29 identifies Jesus as “the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.” John 3:17 likewise says that God sent the Son “that the world should be saved through him.”

The universalist argument is therefore not simply that God loves everyone equally. It is that the New Testament identifies Christ’s work with God’s purpose for the world. The same Christ through whom creation exists is the Christ through whom creation is reconciled.

Yet the New Testament also speaks of a “narrow” way, unbelief, condemnation, and judgment. Christology therefore prevents a simplistic universalism. The saving work of Christ must pass through death and resurrection, faith and transformation, judgment and purification. The question is not whether salvation is cheap but whether Christ’s reign is ultimately effective.

Hebrews presents Jesus as High Priest who “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). His priesthood is not temporary. The sacrificial language reaches its climax in a once-for-all offering. The priest does not offer one sacrifice for a fraction of creation and then retire. The theological direction is toward completion.

The Lamb image in Revelation is even more paradoxical. The one who conquers is the one who was slain. The final victory of God occurs through sacrificial love rather than brute force. Revelation 5 culminates in worship offered by every creature “in heaven, on earth, under the earth, and in the sea.” The universal chorus is not yet identical with universal salvation, but it creates a theological pressure: the telos of history is universal acknowledgment of the Lamb.

“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29, WEB)

If Christ is truly the Savior of the world, the mediator for all, the Lamb who takes away the world’s sin, and the Lord before whom every knee bows, universal reconciliation becomes a Christological possibility that the interpreter must take seriously.

Philippians 2:5–11: The Self-Emptying Christ and Universal Confession

Philippians 2:5–11 begins with an ethical exhortation: “Have this in your mind, which was also in Christ Jesus.”36 The passage is therefore Christological and paraenetic. Paul invokes the story of Christ to shape the church’s conduct.

Verse 6 says that Christ, “existing in the form of God, didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped.” The passage begins from preexistence or divine status and moves downward into self-emptying.

Verse 7 says he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant and becoming in the likeness of men. The emptying is not described as subtraction of deity but as assumption of servanthood.

Verse 8 reaches the depth: “he humbled himself, becoming obedient to death, yes, the death of the cross.” The cross is the nadir of the downward movement.

Verse 9 begins the reversal: “Therefore God also highly exalted him, and gave to him the name which is above every name.” The resurrection and exaltation vindicate the crucified Christ.

Verse 10 contains the universal claim: “that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, on earth, and under the earth.”37 The threefold expression is comprehensive. It evokes Isaiah 45:23, where Yahweh declares that every knee will bow and every tongue will swear allegiance.

Verse 11 continues: “and that every tongue would confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.” The confession of Jesus as Lord is not a private Christian slogan. It is the universal acknowledgment of the one whom God has exalted.

The central exegetical question is whether this confession is voluntary worship or compelled acknowledgment. Isaiah 45 itself contains both judgment and salvation language. The nations are summoned to turn to Yahweh and be saved. The universal confession therefore belongs within the universal sovereignty of God.

The text does not explicitly say “all will be saved.” A cautious exegete should acknowledge that. What it does say is that the entire cosmic order will confess Christ’s lordship, and that this confession glorifies the Father. The universalist argument is that the New Testament’s use of Isaiah’s universal worship oracle creates a strong presumption that the final confession is not merely the forced admission of defeated enemies.

The ethical context matters. Paul invokes the humiliation and exaltation of Christ to tell Christians to renounce status competition. The final universal confession therefore has its roots in the character of Christ’s lordship: the Lord is the crucified servant. His universal reign is not tyranny.

Recent scholarship continues to debate whether the homage of Philippians 2 is voluntary, involuntary, or deliberately ambiguous; the key point for the present study is that the passage unquestionably universalizes the acknowledgment of Jesus’ lordship and deliberately places that acknowledgment within the Isaianic confession of Yahweh’s sovereignty. citeturn0search2

Colossians 1:15–23: All Things Created and All Things Reconciled

Colossians 1:15–23 is one of the New Testament’s most concentrated statements about Christ and creation.38

Verse 15 calls Christ “the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation.” “Firstborn” should not be reduced to “first creature.” Verse 16 supplies the controlling explanation: “For by him all things were created.” The one through whom all things are created cannot easily be placed among the “all things” he creates.

Verse 16 expands the scope: things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers. The list embraces cosmic and spiritual powers. Everything is “through him and for him.”

Verse 17 says, “He is before all things, and in him all things are held together.” Christ is both prior to creation and its continuing ground.

Verse 18 shifts from creation to resurrection and ecclesiology. Christ is “the head of the body, the assembly,” “the beginning,” and “the firstborn from the dead.” The same firstborn language now identifies resurrection priority.

Verse 19 says, “For all the fullness was pleased to dwell in him.” The fullness theme becomes crucial in Colossians because the letter presents Christ as the locus of divine fullness.

Verse 20 is the universalist center: “and through him to reconcile all things to himself, by him, whether things on the earth or things in the heavens, having made peace through the blood of his cross.”39 The phrase “all things” repeats the scope of verse 16. Creation is all things; reconciliation is all things.

The verb apokatallassō is stronger than a vague suggestion of friendliness. It denotes reconciliation or restoration of relationship. The instrument is the cross. Universal reconciliation is therefore not a postscript detached from Christ’s death. It is the cosmic effect Paul attributes to the cross.

Verse 21 narrows the lens: “You, being in past times alienated and enemies in your mind in your evil works, yet now he has reconciled.” The readers are a concrete instance of the cosmic reconciliation announced in verse 20.

Verse 22 says the purpose is to present them holy, blameless, and irreproachable. Reconciliation is therefore transformative and judicial at once.

Verse 23 introduces perseverance: “if indeed you continue in the faith, grounded and steadfast, and not moved away from the hope of the Good News.” This conditional language must not be ignored. The passage holds together cosmic reconciliation and human perseverance.

The strongest universalist observation is structural: the “all things” of creation and the “all things” of reconciliation occupy parallel positions. The text does not say merely that Christ offers reconciliation to all things. It says God reconciles all things through Christ. A cautious reading should still distinguish the universal cosmic claim from the specific pastoral claim about the Colossians’ perseverance. But the cosmic scope is difficult to shrink without textual evidence.

A current scholarly treatment likewise observes that “all things” is repeated across the unit and that the passage moves from the cosmic claim in verses 15–20 to the concrete reconciliation of the readers in verses 21–23. citeturn0search0

Chapter 4. The Doctrine of Salvation

The New Testament uses many images for salvation because no single metaphor is large enough. Grace describes the divine initiative. Calling and election describe God’s summons and choice. Redemption describes liberation by a price. Forgiveness addresses guilt. Justification addresses right standing. Reconciliation addresses alienation. Sanctification addresses transformation. Adoption describes filial identity. Newness of life describes participation in resurrection. Repentance and conversion describe the turning of the human person. Faith describes trust and allegiance.

These images should not be made competitors. Together they form a grammar of restoration.

Grace is prior.7 “For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God” (Ephesians 2:8). The initiative is God’s. If grace is the ground of salvation, then universalism asks whether God’s grace is finally successful in accomplishing what God intends.

Calling and election must be interpreted Christologically. Ephesians 1 says that believers were chosen in Christ “before the foundation of the world” and that God’s purpose is “to bring together in one all things in Christ” (1:10). Election is not presented as God’s arbitrary desire to rescue some while abandoning the rest. It serves the larger purpose of summing up all things in Christ.

Redemption is liberation. Israel’s exodus becomes the controlling background. The redeemed are not merely declared free; they leave bondage and become a people. When Paul speaks of the redemption of creation in Romans 8:21–23, the scope becomes cosmic. Creation itself is waiting for liberation from corruption.

Forgiveness removes guilt, but forgiveness is not the whole of salvation. The prodigal son is not merely acquitted; he is restored to communion. The lost sheep is not merely declared innocent; it is found. The lost coin is recovered. The tax collector is reconciled. Zacchaeus embodies transformed life. Salvation is therefore relational.

Justification in Paul is likewise not a license to remain unchanged. Romans 5 moves from justification to reconciliation and from reconciliation to life. Romans 8 moves from no condemnation to the Spirit’s life-giving presence. Paul can therefore say that those whom God justifies he also glorifies (8:30). The chain is not a list of disconnected options. It describes God’s saving purpose from beginning to end.

Reconciliation is perhaps the most explicit universalist vocabulary.8 “God was in Christ, reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses” (2 Corinthians 5:19). Colossians 1:20 is even broader: through Christ God reconciles “all things to himself.” The Greek ta panta means “all things” and, in context, includes the created order. The church must proclaim reconciliation because God is already acting toward the world through Christ.

Sanctification answers an objection sometimes raised against universalism: what if a person refuses God forever? Scripture treats refusal as a condition that grace can heal. Sanctification is the transformation of desire. Paul prays that believers be sanctified completely (1 Thessalonians 5:23). The Spirit produces fruit. The Christian hope is not that God will tolerate an eternal pocket of rebellion but that God will complete his work.

Adoption reaches the same end through family imagery. Romans 8 speaks of believers receiving the Spirit of adoption and awaiting “the redemption of our body.” The final state is not disembodied individual survival but participation in the family life of God.

Repentance and faith remain essential. Universalism must never be expressed as “it does not matter what you believe.” It matters immensely. Faith is participation in the truth; repentance is the turning of the whole person. Yet if God is finally victorious, the question becomes whether repentance must occur before death or whether the resurrected life can include a final unveiling in which every creature comes to the truth. Scripture does not give a simple chronology. It does, however, portray the risen Christ as the one before whom all will finally stand.

This is where universalism can be distinguished from universalism by mere divine fiat. The Christian doctrine is not that God declares every person saved while leaving evil untouched. It is that Christ’s saving work eventually heals the human creature. Judgment, repentance, purification, and reconciliation belong to the same divine movement.

“But God demonstrates his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.” (Romans 5:8, WEB)

The deepest universalist claim is consequently about the efficacy of grace. If grace can raise the dead, it can heal the will. If Christ can reconcile enemies, the final victory need not be limited by the duration of human resistance.

Romans 5:12–21: Adam, Christ, and the Logic of Much More

Romans 5:12–21 is arguably the most important Pauline text for the universalist doctrine because Paul deliberately constructs an Adam-Christ parallel.32 The passage must be read as an argument, not a collection of isolated “all” statements.

Verse 12 begins: “Therefore, as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin; and so death passed to all men, because all sinned.” The sentence introduces a comparison that Paul does not complete until verse 18. The intervening material explains why Christ’s gift is greater than Adam’s trespass.

Verses 13–14 establish the universality of death even before the Mosaic law. Adam functions as “a foreshadowing of him who was to come.” This is a remarkable statement. Adam’s significance is typological: the first man anticipates Christ, the second Adam.

Verse 15 says, “But the free gift isn’t like the trespass.” This “not like” does not cancel the comparison; it qualifies it. Adam’s trespass brought death, while Christ’s grace brings a gift. Paul repeatedly emphasizes that the gift is “much more.”

Verse 16 contrasts condemnation with justification. One trespass resulted in condemnation; the gift following many trespasses results in justification. The asymmetry is important: Christ does not merely undo one sin. He answers a history of many trespasses.

Verse 17 is stronger still. “For if by the trespass of the one, death reigned through the one; so much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, Jesus Christ.” The phrase “much more” is Paul’s recurring theological logic. Grace is not merely equal to the damage caused by sin. It exceeds it.

Verse 18 gives the fullest parallel: “So then as through one trespass all men were condemned; even so through one act of righteousness all men were justified to life.”33 The two “all men” clauses are grammatically parallel. The question is whether they are coextensive.

Traditional readings often limit the second all by the phrase “those who receive” in verse 17 or by Paul’s broader doctrine of faith. That is a legitimate interpretive consideration. Yet the text itself does not insert a limitation into verse 18. The universalist reading therefore has substantial grammatical grounds for treating the second “all” as genuinely universal.

Verse 19 restates the argument with “many”: “For as through the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the one the many will be made righteous.” Here the contrast between “one” and “many” is explicit. “Many” does not automatically mean “all,” but Paul’s shift between “all” and “many” demonstrates that he can use both terms for the representative groups affected by Adam and Christ.

Verse 20 introduces the law. The law entered “that the trespass might increase.” This does not mean God desired sin for its own sake. Paul is showing that the law reveals and magnifies transgression, making the superabundance of grace even clearer.

Verse 21 provides the telos: “that as sin reigned in death, even so grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.” Sin has a reign. Death has a reign. Grace also has a reign. Paul’s theological question is therefore not merely how many individuals are saved but which reign finally governs humanity.

A universalist reading should not pretend that Romans 5 independently answers every question about faith, judgment, or postmortem salvation. It does something more foundational: it establishes a typological principle in which Christ’s work is not weaker in scope or effectiveness than Adam’s.

The strongest objection is that verse 17 explicitly speaks of “those who receive the abundance of grace.” The universalist answer is that reception describes the mode of participation in the gift rather than a limitation on the ultimate reach of God’s grace. Whether that answer is persuasive depends on how Romans 5 is integrated with Romans 8 and the wider Pauline corpus.

The “much more” language remains decisive. Paul is not interested in presenting Christ as a partial repair of Adam. He presents Christ as the superabundant answer to Adam. Death entered through one; life reigns through one. Sin reigned; grace reigns. Condemnation came through one act; justification leading to life comes through one righteous act.

The universalist therefore reads Romans 5 as a fundamental statement of representative solidarity: humanity is universally implicated in Adam, and Christ’s saving work is correspondingly presented as God’s universal answer to Adamic ruin. Other passages must determine the precise manner and chronology of that answer, but Romans 5 supplies the architecture.

Chapter 5. The Death and Resurrection of Jesus

The cross is the center of Christian theology because it is the place where God’s holiness, human sin, divine love, judgment, and victory converge. Any account of universal salvation that makes the cross unnecessary has misunderstood both universalism and the gospel.

The Passion narratives are restrained. Jesus is betrayed, arrested, tried, mocked, crucified, dies, and is buried. The evangelists do not give one exhaustive theory of how the cross works. Instead they surround the event with scriptural allusion and theological interpretation. Jesus is the suffering servant, the Passover lamb, the righteous sufferer, the king, the Son, and the representative human.

The New Testament’s explanations of the cross are correspondingly plural. Jesus gives his life “as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). His blood is “poured out for many for the remission of sins” (Matthew 26:28). Paul speaks of justification through his blood, reconciliation through his death, redemption, and Christ becoming a curse for us. John emphasizes the Lamb. Hebrews emphasizes priestly sacrifice. First Peter emphasizes Christ bearing sins and bringing us to God.

No single model should be made to carry more weight than the canon gives it. The ransom image points to liberation. Sacrificial language points to covenant and purification. Reconciliation points to restored relationship. Victory language points to conquest over death and the powers. These are complementary.

The resurrection is not an epilogue to the cross.9 It is God’s declaration that death has not won. Paul calls Christ “the first fruits of those who are asleep” (1 Corinthians 15:20). First fruits imply a harvest. Christ’s resurrection is the beginning of a larger resurrection. The resurrection of the body means that salvation reaches the creature at the deepest level.

Paul’s resurrection argument contains one of the strongest universal patterns in the New Testament. “For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive” (1 Corinthians 15:22). The immediate context then distinguishes “each in his own order”: Christ first, then those who are Christ’s at his coming, and finally the consummation when Christ delivers the kingdom to God. The text therefore requires careful handling. It does not say that every person is simultaneously resurrected in the same manner. But it does say that death’s Adamic universality is answered by Christ’s life-giving work.

The final resurrection is also tied to the destruction of death. If the last enemy is death, then the final state cannot contain an everlasting realm whose defining feature is death. Revelation’s lake of fire is a serious problem for simplistic readings, but the book’s own symbolic world identifies death and Hades as cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:14). The narrative then moves to the New Jerusalem and the tree whose leaves are “for the healing of the nations” (22:2).

The resurrection therefore supplies the ontological basis for universal restoration. God does not merely persuade immortal souls. God raises bodies, renews creation, and defeats death. Universalism is most coherent when salvation is understood as resurrection life rather than merely postmortem comfort.

The cross also tells us what God’s judgment looks like.10 God does not overcome evil by becoming evil. The crucified Christ absorbs violence without surrendering love. This is why the cross can be both judgment and mercy. Evil is exposed, condemned, and overcome through self-giving love.

“He is not here, but is risen, as he said. Come, see the place where the Lord was lying.” (Matthew 28:6, WEB)

The universalist hope therefore rests not on optimism about humanity but on God’s act in Christ. If the crucified one is risen, and if the risen one is Lord of the living and the dead, then the horizon of salvation must be measured by resurrection power.

1 Corinthians 15:20–28: Resurrection, the Last Enemy, and God All in All

First Corinthians 15 is Paul’s extended defense of bodily resurrection.3435 Verses 20–28 form the climax of his argument.

Verse 20 declares: “But now Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruits of those who are asleep.” “First fruits” implies sequence. Christ’s resurrection is not an isolated exception but the beginning of a harvest.

Verse 21 grounds the resurrection in the Adam-Christ pattern: “For since death came by man, the resurrection of the dead also came by man.” Paul then states in verse 22: “For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive.”

The symmetry is explicit. “All” dies in Adam; “all” is made alive in Christ. Verse 23 immediately qualifies the sequence: “But each in his own order: Christ the first fruits, then those who are Christ’s, at his coming.” The universalist must therefore avoid claiming that verse 22 means every individual is raised at precisely the same moment or in precisely the same mode. Paul clearly distinguishes orders.

But verse 24 continues toward “the end,” when Christ delivers the Kingdom to God the Father, “when he has abolished all rule and all authority and power.” The purpose of Christ’s reign is the destruction of hostile powers.

Verse 25 says, “For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet.” The phrase “until” is important. Christ’s reign is directed toward a condition in which enemies are subjected.

Verse 26 identifies the final enemy: “Death will be the last enemy to be destroyed.” Death is not merely the place in which unbelievers reside; it is an enemy. If death remains eternally as the defining state of some creatures, the final destruction of death becomes difficult to describe as complete.

Verse 27 explains the scriptural basis by Psalm 8. “He put all things in subjection under his feet.” Paul adds that God is excepted as the one who subjected all things to Christ. The scope is cosmic.

Verse 28 gives the climax: “When all things have been subjected to him, then the Son will also himself be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all.” The phrase “God all in all” is the telos of the argument.

A universalist reading should not make “all in all” do more than it says. Paul is describing the consummation of Christ’s mediatorial reign. Yet the phrase is remarkably comprehensive. It presents God’s presence and rule as the final condition of reality.

If some rational creatures remain eternally opposed to God, then one must explain how God is “all in all” while a permanent realm of rebellion remains outside God’s reconciling purpose. That explanation may be possible, but it cannot simply be assumed. The universalist interpretation has the advantage of taking Paul’s climactic language at its widest scope.

The chapter also makes bodily resurrection essential. Universal salvation cannot mean that immortal souls simply continue elsewhere. The final victory is resurrection, transformation, and the destruction of death.

Chapter 6. The Kingdom of God

Jesus’ central proclamation is the kingdom of God. The kingdom is God’s reign, not merely a place called heaven. It is present in Jesus’ ministry and future in its consummation.

The kingdom is present because the king is present.11 Jesus announces that the kingdom has drawn near. He casts out demons, heals the sick, forgives sins, welcomes outsiders, and raises the dead. These signs are not random miracles; they are manifestations of restored creation.

Yet the kingdom is future. Jesus teaches his disciples to pray, “Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10). The prayer assumes that God’s will is not yet universally realized on earth. The kingdom therefore has an inaugurated and consummated dimension.

The parables of the kingdom frequently emphasize hidden growth, judgment, separation, and final harvest. Wheat and weeds grow together until harvest. The net gathers fish of every kind and then separates. The prodigal is welcomed. The lost sheep is sought. These images resist simplistic eschatology. God’s reign includes judgment, but judgment serves the king’s purpose.

The kingdom and Christ’s reign must be distinguished without being separated.12 First Corinthians 15 says that Christ reigns until he has put all enemies under his feet, and then “the Son will also himself be subjected to the one who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all” (15:28). “God all in all” is one of the strongest telos statements in the canon. It describes not merely political subjection but a condition in which God’s life fills all things.

The phrase “all in all” should be allowed to speak. Paul is not describing a divine victory in which most of creation remains permanently estranged. His language is cosmic. The enemies are defeated; death is destroyed; the Son hands the kingdom to the Father; God becomes all in all.

This does not mean that every distinction disappears. Biblical unity is not uniformity. Revelation retains nations. Paul retains persons. The kingdom heals difference by reconciling it rather than annihilating it.

The kingdom also shapes Christian ethics. If God will finally reconcile creation, the church must become a sign of reconciliation now. Enemies are loved because the kingdom has begun. Forgiveness is practiced because God is forgiving. The Lord’s Prayer teaches Christians to pray for the kingdom while embodying its values.

The question of reigning with Christ also changes the meaning of salvation. Revelation promises participation in Christ’s reign. Paul says the saints will judge the world and even angels (1 Corinthians 6:2–3). The destiny of the redeemed is therefore not passive enjoyment. It is participation in God’s restorative rule.

“Therefore don’t be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the Kingdom.” (Luke 12:32, WEB)

The kingdom gives universalism its proper scale. The question is not merely “How many individuals go to heaven?” It is “What does God intend to do with creation?” The biblical answer is kingdom: Christ reigning until every enemy is defeated, creation liberated, and God all in all.

Chapter 7. The People of God

The church exists because the kingdom has arrived in Christ. Jesus gathers disciples, appoints apostles, and speaks of building his ekklēsia. The church is therefore neither a voluntary association nor merely a religious institution. It is a people called into participation in the Messiah’s life.

The New Testament uses several images. The church is the people of God, the body of Christ, God’s temple, the bride, a household, and the fellowship of the Spirit. Each image implies relationship.

The body metaphor is especially important.13 “Now you are the body of Christ, and individually members of it” (1 Corinthians 12:27). The church does not replace Christ; it participates in Christ. Baptized believers are incorporated into one body by one Spirit. This is why the church’s unity matters.

The church’s existence also has an eschatological purpose.14 It is a preview of reconciled humanity. Galatians 3:28 speaks of Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female becoming one in Christ. Ephesians 2 depicts Jew and Gentile being made into “one new man” through the cross. The church is thus a sign of the reconciliation Christ intends for creation.

The term koinōnia means fellowship, participation, sharing. The Spirit creates communion not only between believers and God but among believers. Acts portrays the first Christians sharing possessions, meals, prayer, and worship. Eucharistic fellowship makes the church a visible sign of the kingdom.

Universalism must not make the church unnecessary. The opposite is true. If God’s purpose is reconciliation, the church is the community called to embody reconciliation in advance. It becomes the laboratory of forgiveness, patience, truth, justice, hospitality, and shared life.

At the same time, the church must not confuse its boundaries with the boundaries of God’s mercy. The New Testament repeatedly surprises insiders by showing God’s grace reaching outsiders. Gentiles receive the Spirit. Samaritans enter the community. Roman soldiers become objects of faith. The church’s mission is therefore expansive.

The church’s witness to universal reconciliation is not a demand that everyone already believe it. Christians have disagreed about the destiny of the wicked across the centuries. The task of the church is to confess what it believes Scripture teaches while treating disputants as fellow members of Christ where the central confession of the gospel remains shared.

The people of God also bear a responsibility to avoid coercion. The gospel invites, summons, warns, and commands. It does not require manipulation. If God’s ultimate victory is the victory of love, Christian proclamation must be consistent with that love.

“Now the multitude of those who believed were of one heart and soul. Not one of them claimed that anything of the things which he possessed was his own, but they had all things in common.” (Acts 4:32, WEB)

The church is therefore an eschatological community in miniature. Its unity is not the final restoration, but it is a sign pointing toward it.

Chapter 8. Baptism: Origin and Meaning

Baptism has antecedents in Jewish purification practices, proselyte initiation, and the baptism of John. Yet Christian baptism receives its definitive meaning from the death and resurrection of Jesus and the gift of the Spirit.

John’s baptism is a baptism of repentance. It announces that Israel must prepare for God’s coming judgment and kingdom. Jesus submits to John’s baptism, not because he needs repentance, but because he identifies himself with the people and enters the vocation of the servant.

Christian baptism is Trinitarian and Christological.15 Matthew 28:19 commands baptism “in the name of the Father, of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.” Acts also speaks of baptism in Jesus’ name. Romans 6 interprets baptism as participation in Christ’s death and resurrection: “We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life” (6:4).

Baptism is therefore not merely a public testimony. It is a sacramental enactment of the gospel. The baptized person is identified with Christ’s death and resurrection.

This has implications for universalism. If baptism represents dying and rising with Christ, then the Christian doctrine of salvation is fundamentally participatory. Salvation is not an external verdict alone. The believer is joined to the crucified and risen one.

At the same time, baptism cannot be turned into a boundary marker that restricts God’s mercy. The New Testament contains complex evidence about baptism and salvation. The thief on the cross is not baptized in the narrative, while Cornelius receives the Spirit before his baptism. The Spirit’s sovereignty exceeds ritual sequencing.

Baptism also anticipates resurrection.16 Colossians 2 connects burial with Christ and being raised through faith in God’s power. First Peter connects baptism with the resurrection of Jesus. The sacrament therefore looks backward to the cross, inward to incorporation, and forward to resurrection.

The practice of baptism also creates a people. The baptized are not isolated consumers of salvation. They enter a community. The church is the place where the baptized learn to live as those who have died to the old age and begun to inhabit the new.

A universalist account should consequently resist both sacramental minimalism and sacramental absolutism. Baptism is precious because Christ is precious. It is a sign and means of participation in his life. But God’s saving work cannot be reduced to the church’s administration of water.

“Therefore we were buried with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life.” (Romans 6:4, WEB)

The final significance of baptism is thus eschatological: it rehearses the passage from death to life that God intends for creation.

Chapter 9. The Lord’s Supper

The Lord’s Supper stands within the story of Passover and covenant. Jesus takes bread and wine and identifies them with his body and blood. The meal is remembrance, proclamation, communion, covenant, and anticipation.

Paul says, “For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Corinthians 11:26). The meal therefore holds together past and future. The church remembers the cross while awaiting consummation.

The Passover background matters. Passover commemorates decisive deliverance. Israel remembers that God brought slaves out of Egypt. Jesus transforms this memory around his own death. The exodus pattern becomes Christological: deliverance occurs through the Messiah.

The Supper is also fellowship in the body of Christ.17 “The cup of blessing which we bless, isn’t it a communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, isn’t it a communion of the body of Christ?” (1 Corinthians 10:16). Communion is not merely private devotion. The many become one body.

This has ethical consequences. Paul rebukes the Corinthians because their Eucharistic practice contradicts their fellowship. The meal that proclaims reconciliation becomes a scandal when the wealthy humiliate the poor. The Lord’s Supper therefore judges the church’s social life.

The Supper also points to the eschatological banquet. Jesus speaks of eating and drinking in the kingdom. Revelation concludes with the wedding supper of the Lamb. The final image is not isolation but feast, communion, and joy.

Universal reconciliation fits this trajectory because the Eucharist is a foretaste of the unity toward which creation moves. Yet it must not be used to erase judgment. Paul warns that unworthy participation has consequences. The sacrament is both comfort and summons.

The new covenant language is crucial.18 Jeremiah 31 promises a covenant in which God’s law is written on hearts and sins are remembered no more. Hebrews identifies this promise with the new covenant inaugurated by Christ. The final covenant is therefore transformative. God does not merely alter legal bookkeeping; God writes his law into human hearts.

This provides another answer to the objection that universal salvation would make moral transformation irrelevant. The new covenant’s universality is precisely the universality of transformed desire. If God writes the law on the heart, then obedience becomes internal rather than externally imposed.

“For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” (1 Corinthians 11:26, WEB)

The Supper is therefore a liturgical theology of universal reconciliation: deliverance has occurred, communion is being enacted, and consummation is coming.

Chapter 10. The Ministry of the Church

The church is a ministering people because Christ is a ministering Lord. Jesus sends disciples to preach, teach, heal, baptize, forgive, and serve. The ministry of the church is therefore derivative: it participates in Christ’s own ministry.

The New Testament names apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers, elders, overseers, and deacons.19 These titles describe functions that serve the community’s growth and mission. The lists are not identical, which suggests that the early church possessed a certain flexibility in organization.

The apostolic ministry is foundational because the apostles bear witness to Christ’s resurrection. Prophets speak into the community. Teachers preserve and explain the faith. Elders oversee. Deacons serve. Whatever the precise historical development of offices, the theological principle is clear: authority exists for edification.

Worship is central to ministry. Romans 12:1 calls believers to present their bodies as a living sacrifice. Worship is therefore not confined to a sanctuary. It is a whole-life response to God’s mercy.

Evangelism is proclamation of good news. The gospel is not “try harder so that God might tolerate you.” It is announcement of what God has done in Christ. Acts repeatedly summarizes the apostolic message as the resurrection and lordship of Jesus, repentance and forgiveness, and the coming kingdom.

A universalist theology must preserve urgency in evangelism. If all will ultimately be reconciled, why preach? Because salvation is not merely a final destination. Reconciliation with God is good now. Freedom from sin is good now. Communion with Christ is good now. The gospel brings people into the life for which they were created.

Paul can also describe himself as a minister of reconciliation.20 “We are therefore ambassadors for Christ, as though God were making his appeal through us” (2 Corinthians 5:20). This is perhaps the clearest missionary text for universal reconciliation. The church announces what God is doing and invites people to participate now.

Teaching and discipline likewise remain necessary. If sin is real and grace transforms, the church must teach holiness. Discipline protects the vulnerable and calls the sinner back. Universalism does not entail permissiveness. A doctrine of final reconciliation can produce a more serious doctrine of present discipleship because it treats God’s goal as the actual healing of the person.

Church government is a secondary matter compared with the church’s essential confession and mission, but authority must be exercised in a cruciform way. Jesus contrasts the domination of Gentile rulers with servant leadership: “Whoever desires to become great among you shall be your servant” (Mark 10:43).

“For the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.” (Mark 10:45, WEB)

Christian ministry therefore cannot be separated from the shape of the gospel. The church serves because Christ serves. It proclaims reconciliation because Christ reconciles. It disciplines because love seeks restoration. It evangelizes because communion with God is the good for which every creature was made.

1 Timothy 2:1–7: Prayer for All, Desire for All, Ransom for All

First Timothy 2:1–7 begins with a command that determines how the rest should be read: “I exhort therefore, first of all, that petitions, prayers, intercessions, and givings of thanks be made for all men.”40 The scope is “all people.”

Verse 2 specifies kings and those in high places. The political setting matters. Christians are instructed to pray for rulers, including rulers who do not belong to the Christian community.

Verse 3 says such prayer is “good and acceptable in the sight of God our Savior.” The title “Savior” anticipates verse 4.

Verse 4 is explicit: God “desires all people to be saved and come to full knowledge of the truth.”41 The Greek construction uses the ordinary plural expression for all human beings. The immediate context therefore favors a genuinely universal reference.

Verse 5 says, “For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, Christ Jesus, himself man.” The unity of God and the unity of the mediator support the universal scope of the preceding verse.

Verse 6 is even more direct: Christ “gave himself as a ransom for all.” The universal word appears again. The ransom is not described as offered merely to a secret subset.

Verse 7 identifies Paul as a preacher and apostle to the Gentiles. The Gentile mission is thus connected to the universal scope of God’s saving purpose.

A common theological response is that “all” can mean all kinds or classes of people rather than every individual. There is contextual reason to consider that possibility because verse 2 specifically mentions rulers and ordinary people. Yet “all kinds of people” remains a possible interpretation, not a necessary lexical meaning. The sequence—pray for all, God desires all to be saved, one God and one mediator, Christ gave himself as ransom for all—creates a remarkably coherent universal horizon.

The text also creates a theological problem if God’s desire for all is permanently frustrated. One can distinguish God’s moral will from God’s decretive will, but the passage does not itself make that distinction. It presents God’s desire for salvation as the ground of universal Christian prayer.

The ransom language should also be taken seriously. A ransom is given for the liberation of captives. If Christ gives himself as ransom for all, the universalist asks whether the ransom’s intended scope is finally effective.

The passage does not explicitly answer the chronology of salvation after death. Nor does it say that all are saved irrespective of faith. It does establish, however, a universal divine saving will and a universal reference for Christ’s self-giving. Chapter 11. The Christian Life

The Christian life is participation in the new age while living within the old. Prayer, stewardship, Sabbath and the Lord’s Day, marriage and family, and the Christian’s relation to the state all become expressions of eschatological identity.

Prayer begins with the Father.21 Jesus teaches disciples to pray, “Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as in heaven” (Matthew 6:9–10). This prayer is universal in aspiration. The Christian asks for God’s reign to become fully effective.

Prayer also teaches trust. If God is the Father, prayer is not an attempt to manipulate an uncertain deity. It is participation in God’s purposes. The universalist may therefore pray confidently for the salvation of all, not because the believer has discovered a loophole in judgment but because God’s revealed character invites hope.

Stewardship follows. Creation belongs to God. Human beings are stewards, not absolute owners. Universal restoration gives stewardship an eschatological depth: creation is not disposable. The Christian cares for the world because the world is God’s.

Sabbath and the Lord’s Day announce that time itself belongs to God. Sabbath resists the illusion that human worth is measured by production. Resurrection worship announces that the new creation has begun.

Marriage and family are treated seriously in the New Testament, but Jesus also relativizes biological family by placing discipleship at the center. Marriage is honored while celibacy is honored. The kingdom transforms ordinary relationships without abolishing them.

Divorce receives hard sayings from Jesus because covenant faithfulness matters. Yet the church must interpret these teachings pastorally, remembering that the same Jesus who speaks with severity also receives sinners and protects the vulnerable.

The Christian and the state are addressed in Romans 13, 1 Peter 2, and Revelation 13.22 These texts must be held together. Romans speaks of governing authority; Revelation warns that political power can become beastly. Christian citizenship is therefore neither automatic political allegiance nor automatic rebellion. The church belongs first to the kingdom of God.

A universalist eschatology can deepen Christian political humility. If history ends in God’s judgment and restoration, Christians need not make any earthly regime the carrier of ultimate salvation. The kingdom is God’s gift.

The Christian life is therefore not an escape from ordinary responsibility. It is the practice of the future in the present. Forgive now because reconciliation is God’s future. Tell the truth now because falsehood has no place in the kingdom. Care for the poor now because God’s kingdom overturns status. Pray for enemies now because Christ died for enemies.

“Therefore, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God.” (1 Corinthians 10:31, WEB)

Universal reconciliation is thus ethically demanding. It does not say that nothing matters. It says everything matters because God intends everything to be brought into the freedom of his glory.

Chapter 12. Eschatology: The Goal of History

Eschatology is not the final chapter of Christianity because it is concerned only with the end. It is the doctrine of the goal toward which every other doctrine moves. Creation has a goal. Israel has a goal. Christ’s incarnation has a goal. The cross and resurrection have a goal. The church has a goal. Christian life has a goal. Scripture calls that goal new creation.

The New Testament holds present and future together. Believers already possess eternal life and yet await resurrection. The kingdom is present and future. Christ is enthroned and yet will return. Salvation is experienced now and consummated later.

The parousia, or coming of Christ, is therefore not merely a timetable event. It is the manifestation of Christ’s lordship. Paul says that at Christ’s coming the dead are raised. The Thessalonians are comforted not by an escape from embodiment but by the promise that the dead will be raised and the living transformed.

The resurrection is central. Christianity does not finally promise immortal souls leaving the body. It promises resurrection. First Corinthians 15 insists that the corruptible puts on incorruption. Philippians 3:21 speaks of Christ transforming the body of our humiliation to be conformed to his glorious body.

Time and eternity require care.23 The Greek aiōn and aiōnios deserve particular attention. Aiōn can denote an age, an epoch, or an age-long reality; aiōnios is related to it. It can certainly describe enduring or eternal realities, but lexical analysis cannot by itself settle every eschatological occurrence. Context determines whether an expression emphasizes duration, quality, belonging to the age to come, or an unending result.

This matters for phrases such as “eternal punishment” in Matthew 25:46. The adjective aiōnios modifies both punishment and life. The traditional argument says that if life is everlasting, punishment must be everlasting in the same temporal sense. The universalist response is that the parallel establishes the eschatological character of both, not necessarily identical duration in every conceptual respect. Moreover, the noun kolasis can denote punishment and, in philosophical and ancient usage, correction or pruning. Neither observation proves universalism. It does show that “eternal punishment” cannot be settled merely by translating aiōnios as “never-ending.”

The judgment sayings of Jesus must be taken with full seriousness.24 Gehenna is a warning. The outer darkness is a warning. The narrow gate is a warning. The separation of sheep and goats is a warning. Revelation’s lake of fire is a warning. Universalism is not allowed to erase these texts. Instead it asks what kind of judgment they describe and how judgment relates to the final victory of God.

One biblical possibility is restorative judgment. Fire can purify as well as destroy. Malachi speaks of a refiner’s fire. First Corinthians 3 speaks of a person’s work being tested by fire, with the person saved “yet so as through fire.” Hebrews speaks of God as a consuming fire. The metaphor is therefore not univocal.

Another possibility is that some judgment language describes irreversible consequences without requiring an everlasting conscious state of torment. Destruction can mean death. The second death is a terrifying image. But if death itself is finally destroyed, the interpreter must ask whether “second death” is the final metaphysical state or an image within the apocalyptic narrative of judgment.

The universalist reading finds its strongest support in texts that explicitly speak of God’s universal saving purpose.25 First Timothy 2:4 says God “desires all people to be saved.” First Timothy 4:10 calls God “the Savior of all men, especially of those who believe.” Titus 2:11 says God’s grace has appeared, “bringing salvation to all men.” John 12:32 says Jesus will draw all people to himself. Romans 5 compares Adam and Christ. First Corinthians 15 compares Adamic death and Christic life. Colossians 1 announces reconciliation of all things. Philippians 2 anticipates every knee bowing and every tongue confessing. Revelation 21–22 presents the nations walking in God’s light and the leaves of the tree for their healing.

The strongest objection is that some New Testament texts appear to teach final exclusion.26 Matthew 7:13–14 speaks of the broad and narrow ways. Matthew 25:46 speaks of eternal punishment. Mark 9 speaks of Gehenna and unquenchable fire. Revelation 14:11 speaks of smoke rising forever and ever. Revelation 20 depicts the lake of fire.

The correct response is not to pretend these texts do not exist. It is to interpret them within the whole canonical story. “Unquenchable” fire means fire that cannot be stopped before it accomplishes its work; it does not logically mean fire that burns forever without consuming its object. “Eternal” punishment may denote punishment belonging to the age to come. “Forever and ever” in apocalyptic language must be read in light of the book’s symbolic use of death, Hades, Babylon, and cosmic imagery. These are interpretive arguments, not shortcuts.

A second line of argument concerns the nature of God’s victory.27 First Corinthians 15:25–28 says Christ reigns until every enemy is subjected, death is destroyed, and God is all in all. If “all in all” is the positive description of the final state, then permanent exclusion would require that a part of creation remain eternally outside the scope of God’s reconciling purpose. Such a conclusion is possible only if the universal language is limited by other texts. The interpreter must therefore weigh the evidence rather than assuming the conclusion.

A third line concerns the character of God.28 Scripture says “God is love” (1 John 4:8). Love is not one divine attribute among others competing for priority; it names God’s character. The God revealed in Jesus seeks enemies, forgives sinners, welcomes prodigals, and prays for persecutors. The final triumph of this God is unlikely to be a negotiated coexistence between eternal love and eternal creaturely hatred.

Yet Christian universalism should remain humble. Scripture does not provide a systematic chart of postmortem repentance. It does not tell us exactly how judgment and purification unfold. It does not permit confidence about every detail of the intermediate state. What it gives is a set of promises and a final horizon.

The Christian hope is therefore not “I know exactly how God will save everyone.” It is “I trust that the God revealed in Jesus Christ will not finally fail in his purpose to reconcile his creation.”

The final chapters of Revelation are decisive in their imagery. The New Jerusalem descends. God dwells with humanity. Death is no more. Mourning is no more. The river of life flows from God’s throne. The tree of life stands in the city, and its leaves are for the healing of the nations. The gates are not shut. The nations walk by its light.

This is not the picture of souls escaping a ruined creation. It is the picture of creation healed.

The last word is therefore not hell but resurrection, not alienation but dwelling, not death but life, not division but communion, not the triumph of sin but the triumph of God.

“Behold, I make all things new.” (Revelation 21:5, WEB)

Christian universalism can thus be stated in its most disciplined form: God, through Jesus Christ and in the Holy Spirit, will finally accomplish the reconciliation and restoration of creation, bringing every creature into the truth and life of God, while judging and overcoming all evil. The doctrine is not proved by one verse. It emerges from the convergence of creation, incarnation, cross, resurrection, kingdom, reconciliation, judgment, and new creation.

Matthew 25:31–46: The Son of Man, the Nations, and the Judgment of the Age

Matthew 25:31–46 is commonly treated as one of the strongest New Testament arguments for everlasting conscious punishment.29 The passage certainly teaches a final judgment, a decisive division, and the seriousness of neglecting the vulnerable. A universalist reading must therefore begin by refusing to domesticate the warning. The question is narrower: what exactly does Matthew’s language establish about the nature and final duration of the punishment?

Verse 31 opens with the temporal marker “But when the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the holy angels with him, then he will sit on the throne of his glory.” The scene is explicitly eschatological. The Son of Man is not merely a teacher offering ethical advice. He is the Danielic figure who receives dominion and judges. The allusion to Daniel 7 is important because the Son of Man’s dominion extends over “all peoples, nations, and languages.” Matthew therefore frames judgment within universal kingship.

Verse 32 says, “Before him all the nations will be gathered, and he will separate them one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats.” The Greek expression panta ta ethnē can mean “all the nations” or, in some contexts, “all the Gentiles.” The word ethnē does not itself mean “every individual human being.” Yet the scene is unmistakably universal in judicial reach. The nations stand before the Messiah.

The separation metaphor is not itself a description of two eternal metaphysical substances. Sheep and goats are separated because the king is exposing the moral reality of their conduct. The next verses explain the criterion.

Verse 34: “Then the King will tell those on his right hand, ‘Come, blessed of my Father, inherit the Kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.’” The kingdom is inheritance, gift, and preparation. The phrase “from the foundation of the world” places the judgment within God’s prior purpose. The righteous are not surprised by a kingdom invented at the end; they enter the purpose for which the kingdom was prepared.

Verse 35 gives the criterion: “For I was hungry, and you gave me food to eat. I was thirsty, and you gave me drink. I was a stranger, and you welcomed me.” Verse 36 continues with nakedness, sickness, and imprisonment. The remarkable feature is that the king identifies himself with the least. This is not a generic moralism detached from Christology. The King says, “you did it to me” in verse 40.

Verse 37 records the righteous asking when they saw the king hungry or thirsty. Their question implies that their mercy was not performed as a calculated attempt to earn reward. Verse 40 gives the interpretive key: “Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.” The identity of “these my brothers” is disputed. Some interpreters identify them specifically with Jesus’ disciples or missionaries; others understand the phrase more broadly. The passage itself clearly makes the treatment of vulnerable persons the arena in which one’s relationship to the king becomes visible.

Verse 41 introduces the negative verdict: “Then he will say also to those on the left hand, ‘Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire which is prepared for the devil and his angels.’” The phrase “prepared for the devil and his angels” matters. The fire is not originally described as a destiny prepared for humanity. It is associated with the devil and his angels. This creates an important theological question: if the devil’s power is finally defeated, must the fire be understood as an everlasting sphere in which defeated evil nevertheless remains permanently successful over human beings? The text does not itself answer that question.

Verse 42 reverses the earlier acts of mercy. “For I was hungry, and you didn’t give me food to eat. I was thirsty, and you didn’t give me a drink.” The judgment is therefore moral and relational. It is not arbitrary punishment.

Verse 45 repeats the verdict: “Most certainly I tell you, because you didn’t do it to one of the least of these, you didn’t do it to me.” The king’s identification with the least means that the judgment reveals what the human subject has become. Judgment is revelatory as well as punitive.

Verse 46 is the crux: “These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life.”30 The Greek nouns are kolasis and zōē, both modified by aiōnios. The adjective is certainly capable of expressing enduring or everlasting duration. It is therefore inadequate to argue that aiōnios simply means “temporary” in every occurrence. At the same time, the semantic question is not exhausted by substituting “everlasting” for the Greek word. Aiōnios is derived from aiōn, and in eschatological contexts it can identify what belongs to the age to come. The noun it modifies must also be considered.

The argument from symmetry is substantial. The same adjective modifies punishment and life. Any interpretation that makes the adjective mean radically different things in the two halves of the sentence requires contextual justification. The universalist therefore should not claim that Matthew 25:46 by itself proves temporary punishment. It does not. The more defensible claim is that the verse identifies both destinies with the coming age and does not, by the adjective alone, settle whether the punitive process is endless, whether its result is endless, or whether punishment is restorative.

The noun kolasis deserves careful attention but not etymological overconfidence. Its verbal family can be associated with pruning or checking growth, and ancient philosophical writers sometimes distinguish kolasis from vengeance. Yet a word’s etymology does not determine its meaning in a later context. Here the noun plainly denotes serious punishment. The universalist case should therefore rest on the larger canonical picture rather than on the claim that kolasis “really means correction.”

There is another contextual observation. Matthew’s Gospel repeatedly presents judgment as related to the exposure and purification of human allegiance. The sheep and goats scene follows the parables of the ten virgins and the talents. Those parables stress readiness, faithfulness, and accountability. Matthew is not giving a technical map of the metaphysics of hell. He is warning disciples that their response to the kingdom has real consequences.

A universalist interpretation can therefore say: Matthew 25:31–46 teaches final judgment, serious punishment, and the necessity of mercy. It does not, standing alone, require the conclusion that God will sustain an everlasting state of creaturely rebellion. The passage must be read alongside Matthew’s larger theology of restoration, especially Jesus’ promise that the Son of Man will sit on a throne and the nations will be gathered before him.

The judgment of Matthew 25 is thus a judgment under the reign of the Messiah. The King is not a lesser power presiding over a permanently divided cosmos. He is the one to whom the nations ultimately belong. That larger Christological fact does not erase the warning; it places the warning within the story of the King’s victory.

Mark 9:42–48: Gehenna, the Little Ones, and the Fire That Is Not Quenched

Mark 9:42–48 begins not with an abstract discussion of the afterlife but with Jesus’ warning about harming “one of these little ones who believe in me.” The immediate setting is discipleship, humility, and the protection of vulnerable believers. The severe imagery is therefore ethical before it is speculative.

Verse 42 says that it would be better for the offender if a great millstone were hung around his neck and he were thrown into the sea. The comparison is intentionally horrifying. Jesus is not giving a measured penalty chart. He is using shocking imagery to communicate the gravity of causing spiritual harm.

Verses 43–47 repeat the command with hand, foot, and eye. The repetition is rhetorically deliberate. The body parts represent whatever within a person’s life becomes an instrument of sin. Jesus’ language is not ordinarily read as a literal command to mutilate oneself. The point is radical renunciation: anything that becomes an occasion of sin must be surrendered rather than protected.

The destination is Gehenna. The term is related to the Valley of Hinnom, a location south of Jerusalem associated in the Old Testament with idolatrous practices and later Jewish judgment imagery. Jesus’ use of the term evokes Isaiah 66:24, where the rebellious are pictured as corpses and where worm and fire signify the horror of divine judgment.

Verse 48 quotes Isaiah almost verbatim: “where their worm doesn’t die, and the fire is not quenched.” This Old Testament background is essential. Isaiah 66:24 does not describe immortal sinners consciously suffering forever. It describes the shameful remains of God’s defeated enemies, with the imagery of worms and fire communicating irreversible divine judgment. The quotation therefore does not straightforwardly settle the question of the duration of conscious torment.

The phrase “unquenchable fire” means fire that cannot be put out. It does not necessarily mean fire that never completes its work. Jeremiah 7:20, for example, speaks of God’s wrath being poured out as an unquenchable fire; the emphasis is on the inability of human beings to stop God’s judgment. Likewise, Ezekiel 20 uses consuming fire imagery for judgment. The adjective describes the fire’s unstoppable character.

The textual history of Mark 9 also deserves mention. Some manuscripts omit verses 44 and 46, leaving verse 48 as the repeated quotation from Isaiah. The textual variant does not change the fundamental teaching because verse 48 is secure enough to carry the image, but it does caution against constructing elaborate doctrine from the triple repetition as though every repeated line necessarily had independent textual status.

The universalist question is therefore not whether Gehenna is serious. It is. The question is whether the metaphor requires everlasting conscious torment. Mark’s citation of Isaiah does not compel that conclusion. It depicts divine judgment using the language of death, disgrace, worms, and fire.

There is also an important canonical irony. Isaiah 66:24 appears at the end of a passage that culminates in the worship of God by “all flesh” (Isaiah 66:23). The nations come to see God’s glory; the final verse depicts the defeated rebels. Mark imports the judgment image into Jesus’ kingdom proclamation. The juxtaposition reminds the interpreter that judgment belongs to a larger restoration narrative.

The universalist reading should therefore retain the warning while refusing to make Gehenna a detached metaphysical geography.31 Jesus’ purpose is to summon disciples away from destructive sin and toward life. The repeated phrase “enter into life” is as important as the warning about Gehenna. The contrast is ethical and eschatological: choose life rather than the path that destroys.

The passage thus supports a robust doctrine of judgment while leaving open the larger question of whether God’s judgment is finally remedial, terminal, or some combination of both. That question must be answered from the whole canon.

Revelation 20–22: Judgment, the Lake of Fire, New Creation, and the Healing of the Nations

Revelation 20–22 must be read as an apocalyptic narrative, not as a literal topographical diagram of the afterlife.42 Its symbols are nevertheless theological claims.

Revelation 20:10 describes the devil being thrown into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and false prophet are. Verse 14 then says, “Death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire. This is the second death, the lake of fire.” This identification is critical. Death itself is consigned to the lake of fire.

If death is an enemy, then the casting of death into the lake signifies its defeat. The lake is therefore not merely a container for immortal evil; it is part of the symbolic portrayal of God’s judgment upon evil and death.

Verse 15 says that if anyone is not found written in the book of life, he is cast into the lake of fire. This is the most severe universalist difficulty in Revelation. The text clearly presents exclusion from the book of life as catastrophic judgment.

But chapter 21 changes the horizon. John sees “a new heaven and a new earth.” The holy city descends from heaven. A voice announces, “Behold, God’s dwelling is with people, and he will dwell with them, and they will be his people.” The goal is not evacuation but divine indwelling.

Revelation 21:4 says, “He will wipe away every tear from their eyes. Death will be no more.” The abolition of death directly fulfills the logic of 20:14.

Verse 5 says, “Behold, I make all things new.” The phrase “all things” should be heard against the cosmic scope of Revelation’s earlier judgments.

Verse 6 promises the water of life to the thirsty. Verse 7 promises inheritance to the one who conquers. Verse 8 then provides a contrasting list of the cowardly, unbelieving, abominable, murderers, sexually immoral, sorcerers, idolaters, and liars, whose “part” is in the lake of fire.

The chapter therefore retains both promise and warning. Universalism cannot simply delete verse 8.

Revelation 21:24, however, says, “The nations will walk in its light. The kings of the earth bring the glory and honor of the nations into it.” The nations that had earlier been aligned with the beast are now depicted in relation to the city. Verse 25 says the gates “will in no way be shut by day, for there will be no night there.” Verse 26 again speaks of the glory and honor of the nations being brought into the city.

Then Revelation 22:2 provides the most intriguing universalist image: “The leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations.”43 The nations are not merely tolerated outside the city. They are the object of healing.

Verse 3 says, “There will be no curse anymore.” The curse associated with Genesis is removed. The servants of God serve him. Verse 4 says they see his face. Verse 5 says they reign forever and ever.

The unresolved question is how Revelation 21–22 relates to those outside the city in 21:8. The book does not explicitly narrate a second judgment after the healing of the nations. Nor does it say that the nations in 22:2 are the same individuals as those in 21:8. The interpreter must therefore resist both overconfidence and despair.

A universalist reading can nevertheless identify a canonical movement: the devil is judged; death and Hades are judged; the old creation passes away; God dwells with humanity; death disappears; the curse disappears; the nations enter the light; and the tree’s leaves heal the nations. The final imagery is therefore restorative even though judgment imagery remains severe.

The key is to distinguish the existence of judgment from the permanence of every judged condition. Revelation itself depicts death being destroyed. A state of death cannot be the final state if death is no more.

The universalist thesis is consequently not that Revelation teaches universal salvation in a single uncomplicated sentence. It is that Revelation’s final chapters place judgment within a larger drama whose endpoint is new creation, the abolition of death, the removal of the curse, divine indwelling, and healing.

Synthesis: Eight Texts and One Canonical Argument

These passages do not all say exactly the same thing. Matthew 25 stresses judgment and mercy. Mark 9 stresses the horror of causing spiritual ruin and the seriousness of Gehenna. Romans 5 presents the Adam-Christ typology. First Corinthians 15 presents resurrection and the destruction of death. Philippians 2 presents universal confession of Christ’s lordship. Colossians 1 presents cosmic reconciliation through the cross. First Timothy 2 presents God’s desire for all to be saved and Christ’s ransom for all. Revelation presents judgment followed by new creation and the healing of the nations.

The exegetical case for universal reconciliation therefore should not depend on making every passage independently teach the same proposition. The case is cumulative. The judgment texts establish the seriousness of evil and the reality of divine judgment. The universal texts establish the scope of God’s saving purpose. The Christological texts identify the agent of reconciliation. The resurrection texts identify the destruction of death as the goal. The apocalyptic texts portray the final removal of death and curse and the restoration of the nations.

The resulting doctrine can be stated with appropriate restraint:44 the New Testament gives substantial grounds for understanding divine judgment as subordinate to God’s final victory over sin and death, and it repeatedly describes that victory in universal or cosmic terms. The strongest universalist reading therefore does not deny judgment. It denies that judgment must be the final defeat of God’s saving purpose.

Conclusion: The Shape of the Gospel

The universalist question becomes distorted when it is reduced to a dispute over the length of punishment. Scripture asks a larger question: What is God doing in Christ?

God creates. Humanity falls. Israel is called. The prophets promise restoration. The Son comes. The kingdom arrives. The cross judges sin. The resurrection defeats death. The Spirit creates the new people of God. The church bears witness. The gospel goes to the nations. Christ returns. The dead are raised. Judgment occurs. Evil is overcome. Creation is renewed. God dwells with humanity. The nations are healed.

Within that narrative, universal reconciliation is not an eccentric appendix. It is a proposal about the narrative’s final verb.

The doctrine should nevertheless be held with exegetical discipline. The Bible’s warnings are real. Judgment is real. Sin is real. The possibility of catastrophic loss in the present is real. Universalism does not mean that salvation is trivial. It means that salvation is ultimately victorious.

The deepest biblical reason for hope is Christ himself. The one through whom all things were made is the one through whom all things are reconciled. The one who bears the world’s sin is the one who rises from death. The one who is lifted up draws all people. The one who is Lord receives universal confession. The one who reigns hands the kingdom to the Father so that God may be all in all.

The Christian therefore hopes not because humanity has earned restoration but because God is faithful.

An Invitation to the Victorious Gospel

If the argument of this book has persuaded you that the gospel is larger, deeper, and more victorious than you once believed, then the proper response is not merely to change your opinion about the doctrine of hell. The proper response is to look again at Jesus Christ.

The Christian hope proclaimed in these pages is not ultimately a theory about what happens to people after death. It is the good news of what God has done, is doing, and will finally accomplish in his Son. The Father has not abandoned his creation. The Son has entered our humanity, borne our sin, died our death, and risen in victory. The Spirit gives life and gathers a people who bear witness to that victory. The end toward which Scripture points is not the triumph of evil, nor the permanent victory of death, but the reconciliation and renewal of all things under the lordship of Christ.

That gospel calls for a response.

If you have never entrusted yourself to Jesus Christ, come to him. Do not wait until you have resolved every theological question. Come as a sinner who needs mercy, as a wounded person who needs healing, as a captive who needs freedom, and as a creature who needs the God who made you. Jesus does not merely offer information about salvation. He calls people to follow him.

Repent of sin. Turn from the ways that destroy life. Confess Jesus Christ as Lord. Trust in the grace of God revealed in his death and resurrection. Receive the forgiveness that God gives freely in Christ. Learn to live by the Spirit. Enter into the fellowship of Christ’s people. Become a person whose life bears witness to the kingdom for which you pray: “Your Kingdom come. Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.”

And if you already believe in Christ, perhaps this book calls you to a deeper confidence in the character of God. The God whom Jesus reveals is not less holy because his mercy is greater than we imagined. His judgment is not less serious because his purpose is ultimately restorative. His grace does not make sin insignificant; grace reveals how completely God intends to overcome it.

Therefore, do not use the hope of universal reconciliation as an excuse for complacency. Let it make you more faithful. If God seeks the salvation of all, learn to love all. If Christ died for the world, refuse to regard any person as beyond the reach of grace. If the risen Christ is Lord of all, let his lordship govern your words, your relationships, your possessions, your sexuality, your work, your treatment of the poor, your forgiveness of enemies, and your life within the church. If God will finally wipe away every tear, become even now a person who wipes away tears.

The victorious gospel also gives the church a reason to preach without fear and to hope without limit. We do not proclaim Christ because we have been entrusted with a message of despair concerning the majority of humanity. We proclaim him because in him we encounter the Savior of the world, the Lamb of God, the risen Lord, and the one through whom God is reconciling all things.

This hope should produce humility. We have not discovered a doctrine by which we may congratulate ourselves and condemn those who disagree. We have been invited to trust the character of the God revealed in Jesus Christ. Christians may continue to disagree about the interpretation of particular judgment passages, and they should do so with seriousness, charity, and reverence for Scripture. But whatever conclusions we reach about the final scope of salvation, the church must never cease to proclaim Christ crucified and risen.

So the invitation is simple.

Come to Christ.

Trust his grace.

Follow him in the way of love.

Receive his forgiveness.

Join his people.

Bear witness to his kingdom.

And hope in the God who raises the dead.

The final word of the Christian gospel is not that sin is stronger than grace, that death is stronger than life, or that evil will possess an eternal monument to its victory. The final word is Jesus Christ.

He is Lord.

He is risen.

He reigns.

And because he reigns, the church may hope.

“Behold, I make all things new.” (Revelation 21:5, WEB)
